# Is Your App Worth Building? AI Validation Guide
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/app-worth-building-ai-validation-guide
**Track:** Build to Launch
**Published:** 2026-02-04

## Summary

This article is a case study in compressing market validation from weeks of manual browsing into a 70-minute AI-assisted research sprint, told through Jenny's own decision to build a deal-aggregator app after relocating cross-country and finding nothing that fit her needs. Before writing a line of the app, she used a three-tool stack — Perplexity for cited industry facts, Notion for structuring findings into a database, and Claude Code running parallel agents for actually testing technical data access — to map sixteen deal-related sites and decide whether the idea was worth building at all. The result: seven sites with working RSS feeds or APIs (goldmines), six sites that were blocked or otherwise inaccessible, and a clear enough gap in the English-language market to justify shipping something, even if only her own family ended up using it.

The market itself breaks into four categories with very different data postures, which the article treats as a transferable lesson rather than domain trivia. Deal aggregator sites (Slickdeals, Hip2Save, Ben's Bargains, Dealnews, and others) turned out to be the goldmine specifically because most run on WordPress and expose their content through RSS feeds or the WordPress REST API by default — Hip2Save's API, for instance, returns structured JSON with titles, prices, images, and categories with no special access needed. Cashback platforms (Rakuten, TopCashback, Ibotta) are closed ecosystems by design, protecting their retailer commission relationships with no public APIs. Coupon-extension sites (Honey, RetailMeNot) are actively hostile to automated access, and the article flags Honey specifically, noting PayPal's $4 billion acquisition of it was followed by class-action lawsuits alleging the extension swapped out creators' own affiliate tracking tags for its own. Price trackers like CamelCamelCamel and Keepa are complementary rather than competitive with aggregators, but increasingly gated behind Cloudflare protection or paid APIs.

The article extracts several general lessons from this specific research pass. RSS, a technology from 1999, still quietly powers most of the accessible data in this space, and the article argues this generalizes well beyond deals — blogs, podcasts, job boards, and government data all lean on the same simple feed format. Because roughly 43% of the web runs WordPress, and WordPress exposes a REST API by default, any target site built on it is effectively already API-accessible without special integration work. Anti-bot measures are real and increasing on some sites, but the practical takeaway is not to fight the locked ones — enough open sources typically exist to build something useful without ever touching a hostile target. And critically, the research itself functioned as the validation, not merely preparation for it: had the data been fully locked down, the project wouldn't have been worth pursuing at all, and finding it wasn't is what justified building.

The methodology section details how the 70 minutes actually broke down: roughly 10 minutes defining a research schema, 15 minutes mapping the landscape via Perplexity, then three separate batches of Claude Code agents run in parallel — three agents doing technical audits (checking robots.txt files, testing RSS URLs, probing WordPress endpoints) in about 5 minutes, four agents researching business models by category in about 20 minutes, and two agents doing deeper API testing (fetching real feed data and evaluating field quality) in about 5 minutes — followed by 15 minutes of synthesis. All nine agents combined ran in under 30 minutes of actual runtime. The article frames the data-access hierarchy worth checking, in order, as RSS feeds first, then the WordPress REST API, then robots.txt for explicit blocks, and recommends skipping any site protected by Cloudflare or heavy anti-bot measures rather than trying to work around it.

The second half of the article covers what it took to turn the validated idea into a working app, dealist.space, pulling from six sources simultaneously. The most substantial engineering problem was deduplication — the same product frequently got posted by multiple sources within hours of each other, solved by extracting the Amazon ASIN (a 10-character product identifier embedded in every Amazon URL) and suppressing repeat postings of the same ASIN within a 48-hour window while still allowing it back in for a genuinely new sale cycle. A Chinese-language source required a translation layer that preserved structured deal information (prices, coupon codes, store names) rather than just translating prose, and a timezone bug where deals from a Pacific-time source without explicit timezone metadata were showing up eight hours off had to be fixed by assuming Pacific time as the default for that source. The article closes by generalizing the whole approach into a repeatable framework — the same three-tool stack and data-access hierarchy applied to SaaS directories, job boards, newsletter platforms, API marketplaces, or any other ecosystem — with a 30-minute starter action plan for a reader to run the same process on their own idea.
