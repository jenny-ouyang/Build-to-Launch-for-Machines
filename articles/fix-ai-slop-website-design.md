# How to Stop Your Website From Looking Like AI Slop
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/fix-ai-slop-website-design
**Track:** Products Built and Shipped
**Published:** 2026-10-28

## Summary

The article opens from a domain-registration statistic (411.6 million registered domains as of Q3 2026, growth up from roughly 1% a year to 8.7%, credited to AI build tools) to argue that building a site stopped being the bottleneck, so Jenny went looking for what replaced it as the tell. She audited 120 live pages built with tools like Lovable, Bolt, Replit, Base44, Claude Code and Cursor, scoring each against 16 recurring habits, mostly read by a script off the page's own styles, with a handful of visually-only cues counted by eye. Only 20 of 120 pages showed two or fewer. The piece ranks the ten most common habits by the share of pages carrying them, covering layout choices (three or four-card feature grids with a top icon, a single Lucide icon set, unmodified shadcn component colors aside from the button, no chosen font or a default Inter, a centered headline, a small pill badge above it) and visual flourishes (one accent-colored word in the headline, uppercase wide-tracked labels, stock "AI" icons like lightning bolts and sparkles). The piece's central statistical claim is that no individual habit is damning, since human-made sites share plenty of them too, but 57 of the 120 pages stacked six or more at once, and that density is what reads as generic rather than any single choice.

A second pass sorts the same 120 pages into four recognizable "flavors" built around color and surface treatment: purple-gradient SaaS, dark glass with frosted panels and glow, bento-grid boxes of uneven size, and a newer cream-and-serif look that builders reach for specifically to escape the purple joke. The piece notes that purple, despite the running gag, only showed up on 13% of the 91 main buttons it tallied, with orange, coral and red combined claiming roughly the same share. Underneath whichever flavor sits a stable set of structural defaults — the centered hero, the icon-topped cards, the single colored headline word, the badge above it — so swapping the color palette just moves a page to a different flavor without touching what actually makes it look templated. A parallel list of six motion habits (cards fading in one by one, hover-triggered card lift, numbers counting up from zero, letter-by-letter headline typing, a glowing blob behind an input, slow page-transition slides) makes the same point about animation: these are additions an AI builder reaches for by default when asked to make a page "feel alive," regardless of whether the motion serves anything, and on a slow connection it's often the actual content that arrives last behind the effects.

Jenny grounds the stakes in three outside findings rather than asserting them on her own authority: a security-warning habituation study using fMRI and eye-tracking that found attention to a repeated warning erodes with exposure and recovers only when the warning's appearance changes; a 16-experiment, roughly 27,500-person study finding that stories labeled as AI-assisted were rated about 6.2% lower regardless of how the disclosure was framed; and a separate effort-based explanation holding that work perceived as requiring less effort gets liked less, independent of actual quality. She's explicit that none of the underlying research tested a website specifically carrying an "AI-made" look, and that other work on site design shows audiences often prefer a layout that looks typical for its category, so the reframe she lands on isn't that familiar patterns are bad, it's that every one of the sixteen habits used to be a deliberate, effortful design choice before it became a free default, the same arc she'd already traced through AI writing patterns (where banning the word "delve" just shifted the tell elsewhere rather than removing it). Her proposed dividing line is whether a given habit was actually chosen for a reason or simply arrived because nobody overrode the tool's default.

What counts as a deliberate choice, the article argues, depends on the page's job, so it proposes six site families — landing page, content site, directory, dashboard, interactive tool, learning app — each with its own normal amount of color, motion and layout discipline (a content site needs almost no motion because the text is the product; a learning app uses the most because a moving reward is part of the mechanic; a directory should let the listed items carry color while the frame stays quiet). Jenny illustrates all six with her own shipped products rather than hypotheticals: the buildtolaunch.ai landing page unifying her four subdomains, the vibecoding.builders guides as the content site, the vibecoding.builders project directory, the Build to Launch community dashboard, a second-brain knowledge map inside her own MCP (cited at 516 items and 682 links) as the interactive tool, and Mandarineer, a Mandarin-learning app she built for her kids after finding Duolingo's early lessons too repetitive, as the learning app. Each gets a described before/after, with the directory and dashboard examples specifically calling out badges, stars and per-card buttons being stripped back so the actual listed items stand out instead of the chrome around them.

The fix section describes, without reproducing, a paid eight-step guided workflow inside Jenny's Build to Launch MCP ("Design my UI") that an AI coding tool runs against a connected project: naming the page's purpose from a bank of defined purposes and its family, selecting a visual style against a set of style references and brand specimens, reviewing each UI component against an AI-default-versus-chosen comparison, choosing among layout options scoped to that page family, setting a motion level matched to the family, locking in brand colors against seven fixed design tokens (text size, line length, line height, type scale, spacing, contrast, fonts), building the page from a matched template, and finally running a checker that scores the live page against the tells catalog with a separate AI grader auditing the build, distinct from whatever AI built it. Jenny reports running the check tool against her own buildtolaunch.ai front door and getting four tells back. The piece's completion bar is concrete rather than aesthetic: two or fewer unexamined default signs remaining with a stated reason for each one kept, no horizontal scroll at 390px, body text contrast at 4.5:1 or better, and the page's main action verified to actually work end to end. The verdict isn't that AI-built defaults are inherently bad or that builders should strip every one out; it's that a page should end up carrying only the signs its owner actually chose, with everything else replaced on purpose.
