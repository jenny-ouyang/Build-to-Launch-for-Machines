# Start using /grill-me: get 10x better results with AI
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/grill-me-skill-better-ai-results
**Track:** AI Builders Playbook
**Published:** 2026-08-05

## Summary

Jenny opens by tracing the sudden popularity of Matt Pocock's `grill-me` skill — a handful of lines of instruction that had reached hundreds of thousands of installs on skills.sh by the time she wrote this — and sets out to reverse-engineer why something so short worked so well. Her answer is that its power comes from reordering the collaboration, not from any clever wording. The default AI workflow lets a person describe an idea, has the AI silently fill in the missing decisions, lets it build on those guesses, and only surfaces the wrong assumptions after the work already exists to correct. `grill-me` inverts that sequence: it forces the AI to map out the decision tree behind a stated goal, interview the human about each unresolved branch one question at a time, propose its own recommended answer to force it to take a position, and — critically — only ask about what can't be retrieved from the environment itself, sending it off to check the codebase first whenever a question is actually a fact rather than a judgment call. She breaks down why each part of that structure matters: relentless, exhaustive questioning gives the interview real depth instead of a token round of clarification; resolving decisions in dependency order stops the AI from asking about details that a not-yet-made upstream choice would change; requiring a recommended answer stops a bare question from just dumping cognitive work back onto the human; and pacing to one question at a time protects the feedback loop, since an early answer can make several later questions moot. The skill's newer successor, `grilling`, keeps all of that structure while widening the fact-finding step beyond the codebase to the surrounding environment, but the core discipline is unchanged — the AI is not permitted to act on an assumption it hasn't confirmed with the human.

She generalizes the pattern past coding to writing, teaching, research, and product work, arguing it transfers because every field has decisions that determine everything downstream of them, and cites Matt's own account of session complexity scaling from a handful of questions for a small feature up into the dozens for something bigger. Once the decisions from a `grill-me` session are settled, they still need somewhere durable to live, which is the job of a companion skill called `to-spec`: it reads the whole conversation and produces one specification that preserves the durable parts — the stated problem, the desired result, user stories, scope boundaries — while deliberately dropping fast-changing details like exact file paths or code, on the theory that implementation details age faster than decisions and shouldn't be frozen into a spec. Jenny cites her own experience turning one settled video-pipeline conversation into a spec that fanned out into thirteen separate, independently workable GitHub issues, as the concrete payoff of separating "what was decided" from "what the code currently looks like."

A separate section pulls out the concept of a "deep module" from one of Matt's engineering skills — an interface that exposes a small, stable surface while hiding a large amount of useful behavior behind it, as opposed to a shallow module whose interface is nearly as complex as its implementation. Jenny connects this directly to working with AI agents: every public method, helper, or cross-file convention is something an agent has to hold in its own context, so a deep module gives an agent a clean seam to reason about and test, letting the implementation change behind it without every caller needing to change too. She offers a practical "deletion test" for judging depth — if removing a module forces many callers to recreate its behavior themselves, it had real depth; if removing it just means deleting a few pass-through calls, it was shallow all along.

The middle of the piece organizes eight of Matt's skills under four underlying problems rather than treating them as a flat list. The first problem, controlling the next move, is handled by `writing-beats`, `writing-shape`, and `teach` — all of which read the current state of unstructured material and only ever propose one bounded next step, stopping for a human decision before continuing; `writing-beats` works at the level of individual narrative moves and enforces strict "grounding" so no beat can lean on a concept the reader hasn't been given yet, while `writing-shape` works at the paragraph level and uses a removal test that Jenny found, in practice, can push an article toward over-compression — she describes catching it deleting reasoning steps she actually wanted a learner to see, and had to add an explicit instruction to preserve the causal chain rather than just the conclusion. `teach` solves a related but distinct problem: it builds a persistent, cross-session workspace that tracks a learner's mission, trusted sources, one lesson at a time, and a learning record, and it only writes that record once the learner demonstrates evidence of real change — recall beyond simple recognition, a corrected misconception — rather than crediting a lesson just because the material was shown.

The second problem, separating facts from judgment, is handled by a deliberately minimal `research` skill that dispatches a background agent to investigate one question against primary sources and hands back a single cited note without making any decisions itself, paired with `domain-modeling`, which surfaces unstable or conflicting terminology (her example: two people using "account" to mean different things) and forces a human to pick one canonical meaning, recorded in a plain-language glossary file — escalating to a full architecture decision record only when a choice is hard to reverse, would genuinely surprise a future reader, and reflects a real tradeoff, so that the record stays reserved for consequential choices instead of turning into undifferentiated sediment. The third problem, making failure observable before changing anything, pairs `tdd`, which insists on agreeing a public interface seam and getting the expected test value from an independent source (to avoid what Matt calls a tautological test, where a wrong formula in the code and the test agree with each other), with `diagnosing-bugs`, which insists on first building one fast, reliably reproducible failing case, then stripping it down to only the inputs that actually matter, before ranking falsifiable hypotheses about the cause. Jenny applies this exact method to a real bug in her own "Trim" article-shortening skill, where its safety check was supposed to block any edit that deleted an important number but missed a case where plain numbers like "40" and "2" were silently dropped — she captured that as a permanent regression test and fixed the checker to compare every number before and after an edit. The fourth problem, keeping quality judgments separate, is handled by `code-review`, which evaluates one fixed diff through two independent passes — a Standards review against the repository's own documented rules, and a Spec review against the originating requirements — and deliberately refuses to collapse the two verdicts into a single score, since code can satisfy one axis while failing the other; she extends the same separation to her own writing (arguing quality versus matching her voice) and to her own skills (does the skill do its stated job versus does it follow the meta-rules for how a skill should be built).

The article closes with two reusable pieces. The first is a two-pass prompting pattern she built from all this: an initial pass that runs an exhaustive `grill-me`-style interview until every required decision is either resolved, assigned to fact-finding, or explicitly marked out of scope and produces one decision summary awaiting confirmation, followed by a second pass that branches into a writing, research, or software track, each of which turns the confirmed decisions into a different durable artifact — an approved outline, one cited research note, or an authorized specification — without reproducing the exact prompt wording sold with the piece. The second is a simple four-step recipe for converting a correction she finds herself repeating into a reusable skill: name the correction, name the professional principle hiding inside it, encode the sequence, ownership, and boundary explicitly, and define one observable test that would fail if the AI ignored the rule.
