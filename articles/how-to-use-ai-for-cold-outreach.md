# How to Use AI for Cold Outreach Without Sounding Like Spam
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/how-to-use-ai-for-cold-outreach
**Track:** Products Built and Shipped
**Published:** 2026-10-12

## Summary

This is a recap of a live monthly AMA where four paying members brought outreach problems from four different fields: an independent recruiter building two pipelines, someone selling AI services to small-business owners, a health creator scoping a niche before launch, and a Substack writer trying to grow with limited hours. Jenny answers each in turn, but frames the whole session around one discovery she makes partway through: all four questions are really the same question in different clothes — how do you get a stranger to say yes — and all four answers share the same shape, research the field, research the person, then give something useful before you ask for anything.

For the recruiter, the piece lays out a six-step system split into two pipelines that live in one shared folder of markdown and CSV files: a role-card pipeline that researches one open job and the hiring manager behind it, and a candidate pipeline that scores a pool against weighted criteria and produces a ranked shortlist capped deliberately at ten names, on the logic that mailing everyone is what makes outreach read as spam. Before any pipeline runs, Jenny has the AI research the mechanics of recruiting itself — fee structures, legal limits on contact, what candidates say about recruiter messages — against a set of guesses she writes down first, so the research comes back correcting specific claims rather than producing a generic overview. She cites findings from that research pass, including that the bulk of real placements come from candidates already sitting in a recruiter's own database rather than from cold lists, which reframes where the outreach effort should go first. A separate pass extracts a reusable "voice" profile from the recruiter's own past messages that got replies, so AI-drafted notes keep the same rhythm and vocabulary instead of reading as generic AI polish, and every draft is checked against a short list of banned phrases and a human-read-before-send rule.

The small-business and health-creator answers compress the same research-first logic into a narrower move. For selling AI services to owners in the one-to-two-million-revenue range, her answer is to lead with free value rather than a pitch — she recounts being won over by a cold outreach message that included a Loom video diagnosing a specific flaw in one of her own videos, and generalizes from it into a sequence: understand the demographic's trust gap with AI tools before writing anything, give real value before the first call and more after it, and build local trust in person (citing her own chamber-of-commerce experience) rather than trying to automate the first touch. For the health creator, the method is to build a long list of niche creators, pick one and study their path and offers in depth, and then read the comments under that creator's content, where she finds an unmet need stated outright by a real follower — the comments function as a free signal for where a gap in the market actually is.

The Substack-growth answer trades frameworks for her own numbers: she estimates a minimum of five hours a week is needed to grow meaningfully and says she spends closer to twenty, mostly on writing and research rather than promotion. She contrasts two recommendation swaps with opposite outcomes — a swap with a similarly-sized peer newsletter returned over forty new subscribers in two days, while a swap with a newsletter above 100,000 subscribers returned only three for the thirty-six she sent back — and notes that most of her short-form Notes converted zero paid members while a single long-form guide brought in fifty free and three paid readers, an argument for spending limited hours on depth over frequency.

Jenny closes by naming the pattern explicitly across all four cases and extending it past outreach into other asks — a job search, a guest-post pitch, a partnership proposal, a product launch — arguing each works the same way: research the field and the specific person, lead with something useful, and accept that silence is an acceptable outcome when the fit genuinely isn't there. The piece's standing offer is a single starting action: pick one real person to reach this week, have AI research them in depth, read the report yourself, and find one concrete thing you can give before asking for anything in return.
