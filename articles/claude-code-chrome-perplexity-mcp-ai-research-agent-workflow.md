# Perplexity + Claude in Chrome: How I Built a Competitor Research Workflow in 2 Hours

**Track:** AI Builders Playbook
**Published:** 2026-04-08

## Summary

This article starts from a specific frustration: after months of publishing Gumroad products, Jenny kept second-guessing her cover designs with no real baseline, because the normal way of checking — opening a pile of competitor tabs and going with your gut — quietly collapses under the limits of short-term memory. By the second research session she couldn't remember what the eighth tab looked like, and every design or pricing decision she made afterward was really coming from a handful of remembered fragments rather than the actual market. The fix she built combines Perplexity MCP (for live, current web search rather than a model's stale training data) with Claude in Chrome (for reading live browser pages directly) into a pipeline that scanned 177 products across five marketplaces — Gumroad, App Store, Amazon KDP, Etsy, and Creative Market — in about three hours, and consolidated all of it into a single scored, filterable HTML board she could see at a glance.

The article frames the value of this setup in four layers, and argues the least interesting one is the one people notice first. Speed is table stakes: one AI session can scan a range of products far faster than manual browsing. Delegation is the next layer — once an agent can be sent to go collect while she keeps thinking, her role shifted from doing the collecting to directing where the collection should go next. The third layer, "the loop," is the back-and-forth where the system returns information, she reacts, it fetches more based on that reaction, and the next move becomes obvious — closer to debriefing with someone who already did a first pass than to querying a vending machine. The fourth and most surprising layer is orientation: within a single session she had a mental model of an entire market's shape — where things clustered, what looked strong versus weak, where prices concentrated — that would normally take much longer to earn, and she argues this was still real learning, just compressed rather than slow.

The concrete findings from the 177-product board are where the piece gets specific. Across 52 Gumroad products, covers using the creator's own photo — even an unpolished one, in one case looking like a phone selfie — consistently outperformed cleaner, more polished, photo-free designs, which she reads as a trust signal (a face signals accountability and who you're buying from) rather than a beauty signal. The board also surfaced that clear, literal "what's inside" titles beat vaguer, prettier ones, that some outlier products sold well purely on niche specificity or audience fit rather than design quality, and that visible pricing clusters exposed gaps once the full range was laid out side by side — patterns she says she would never have trusted seeing one product at a time, but that became obvious in aggregate. Turning the lens on her own products, the board sorted strong performers into two buckets — "raw human" (face and personality forward, trust as the primary signal) and "bold/direct" (no hedging, the design statement carries itself) — and she recognized her own products already sat in the second bucket by accident, while a separate finding, that gradients on her covers were making them read as cheaper than the underlying structure deserved, sent her into a deliberate design-education pass on typography and restraint that she hadn't planned to have.

The back half of the article is the reproducible build: setting up Perplexity MCP without an API key by lifting a Perplexity Pro/Max session token from the browser's cookies and wiring it into the MCP config (with the caveat that the token expires and needs periodic refreshing), pairing Claude in Chrome so Claude Desktop can read whatever product page is open in the browser (useful especially for platforms like Etsy and Creative Market that block scraping), and a five-step workflow: prompt the AI to pull 50+ products per platform including underperformers (not just top performers, since weak products supply the contrast that makes patterns visible), collect cover images via an `og:image` scraper for platforms that expose it and an Amazon-specific ASIN-based image URL workaround for the ones that don't, assemble everything into one self-contained HTML board via a small Python script, read the board against four fixed questions (what covers dominate by category, where do prices cluster, which titles explain themselves instantly, which underperforming-looking products still sell), and run the different platform searches in parallel shell processes so the 177-product pull takes hours rather than a week.

The article's overall argument is that the real contrast isn't "research versus just doing the work" — it's fragmented research (which leaves you with vibes) versus decision-grade research (which leaves you with a specific constraint, a direction, and often one uncomfortable truth about your own product). It positions this as the throughline for Month 1 of her Practical AI Builder program: research isn't homework to complete before building, it's the thing you run before any meaningful decision so you stop building from partial memory.
