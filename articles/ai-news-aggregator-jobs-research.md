# How to Make AI Collect Information for You (One Connector, Every Source)
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/ai-news-aggregator-jobs-research
**Track:** Products Built and Shipped
**Published:** 2026-11-15

## Summary

This is a recap of a live monthly AMA where Jenny Ouyang answered three member questions that all turned out to be the same underlying job: getting an AI agent to collect scattered information on a recurring basis instead of a person opening the same tabs every week. Before recommending any tool, she walks through a three-question framework: where does the information actually live and is it gated behind a login or openly published, where should the finished result land (a folder, an email, a spreadsheet), and how often does the job need to repeat. The answer to the first question determines the connector — an inbox source calls for something that reads email, a logged-in source calls for a browser-driving tool, and an open web page calls for a structured search tool — and the piece argues that this matching step, not the AI reasoning itself, is the part of these projects that normally stalls people. To remove that friction she introduces the Build to Launch MCP's "buddies," a library of roughly 130 small hosted tools that each read one public data source so a builder does not have to install, authenticate, and maintain a separate connector per site.

The first case is a longevity-focused newsletter writer who wants a weekly short list of what trusted authors published, each item tagged high, medium, or low relevance. Jenny has Claude survey the field with a topic-matching buddy, which returns 20 candidate newsletters with authors and subscriber counts, and she narrows that to three for the demo. A second buddy then pulls the past week's posts from just those three sources and applies a tagging rule written directly into the prompt, and the live run is used to show the system's honesty as much as its reach: one of the three newsletters had posted nothing since early August, so it correctly produced zero results rather than padding the list, and the piece notes that any buddy reading public pages only ever sees the free portion of a paywalled post. The second case splits a job seeker's and a business owner's requests into one combined problem — collect only new job listings from target companies' career pages, and keep the job running even after the person's own computer is off. Three different buddies are compared head to head: one reading structured job boards (Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby), one reading LinkedIn hiring posts, and one doing freeform web search for fresh listings, and the comparison itself catches a real gap, since the structured job-board search returned zero senior openings at Anthropic while the freeform web search surfaced one the filter had missed. The piece treats that mismatch as the reason to run more than one source rather than trusting any single connector, and it closes the loop on the "computer off" half of the question by moving the recurring search from a local scheduled task to a cloud-hosted routine, since only a cloud routine keeps the user's connectors alive when their own machine is powered down — with the caveat that a cloud run has no local folder to save into, so the destination (run history, a repo, a shared drive, an email) has to be chosen deliberately.

The third case is pre-outreach research on a person and their company, demonstrated live on a public figure (Alex Hormozi and his company) specifically because a famous, heavily documented subject makes every claim easy to verify against a source. Jenny is explicit about a boundary here: LinkedIn's own user agreement prohibits scraping through a logged-in personal account, so the research buddies instead read public LinkedIn pages from Build to Launch's own infrastructure rather than through the user's account, keeping the user's login out of the picture entirely. The research sequence Claude proposes runs in a set order — a general search first, then the subject's other social profiles, then their LinkedIn page, then the company's own page and recent news — and each fact the model surfaces in the final profile is paired with the specific source page it came from, including an identified AI-related staff initiative at the company that became the stated reason to reach out now. When Jenny didn't know which buddy to reach for next, she let Claude choose, and it picked a broader company-site crawl that mapped roughly 80 pages by topic, which she frames as a case where deferring the choice beat a narrower, self-directed request.

Stepping back, the piece names the pattern across all three questions explicitly: each one is fundamentally a data-collection problem, and in every case the hard, tedious part was identifying which connector could reach the source at all — the reasoning and formatting that came after was comparatively easy. With time left in the session, Jenny runs a few more buddies informally: a keyword-suggestion tool that surfaces real search phrasing, a keyword-volume-and-difficulty tool used to prioritize topics, a Wikipedia pageviews buddy used to gauge rising interest in a topic over time, and finally an audit of her own site, buildtolaunch.ai, benchmarked against competitor domains on Ahrefs' Domain Rating scale and checked for technical health (fast load time, strong on-page SEO, a thin structured-data footprint) — a check she reports cost about thirteen cents to run and found her own domain authority meaningfully behind larger newsletter competitors. The piece ends with a direct call to action rather than a tutorial recap: pick one thing currently collected by hand, connect the BTL MCP, run the exact sentence manually once to confirm the output is right, and only then put it on a schedule.
