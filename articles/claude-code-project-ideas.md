# Claude Code Project Ideas
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/claude-code-project-ideas
**Track:** Build to Launch
**Published:** 2026-02-18

## Summary

This article answers the single most common question Jenny got after publishing her Claude Code beginner guide — not "how do I install it" but "what do I actually build with it" — by laying out 15 projects in an explicit four-tier progression, each with a difficulty rating, a time estimate, a copy-paste first prompt, and what skill it's meant to teach. The organizing idea is that the specific projects matter less than the reflexes they build, since models and platforms will keep changing while the underlying patterns (file manipulation, API integration, data storage, workflow automation) stay useful indefinitely. Rather than sorting purely by difficulty, the article also offers a goal-based entry point — pointing a reader who wants an online presence to Project 1, someone drowning in newsletters to Project 5, someone who wants to ship something people pay for to Projects 5 through 8 in sequence, and warns explicitly against skipping straight to payments.

Tier 1 covers four beginner, fully local, zero-cost projects that intentionally never leave the user's machine: a personal website built from existing bio and resume files, a private expense tracker built from bank statement files with interactive category charts, a "daily inspiration" generator that resurfaces a user's own saved notes and quotes each morning, and a photo organizer that renames and sorts a camera roll using file metadata. Jenny is explicit that these four are not optional warmups — each teaches a distinct reflex (file creation, data visualization, text processing, batch automation), and treating them as busywork to rush through undermines the confidence needed for what comes next. She flags her own mistake here directly: builders who rush Tier 1 tend to watch Claude build rather than feel like they built something themselves.

Tier 2 breaks out of the local sandbox into APIs, databases, and deployment. Project 5 is an information-digest app that fetches and AI-summarizes RSS/newsletter sources, introducing API-key authentication for the first time. Project 6 takes the Tier 1 personal website and extends it into a full-stack business hub — RSS-fed content, a database, an AI assistant answering visitor questions, Stripe payments, and calendar booking. Project 7 is a multi-source data-intelligence dashboard pulling from GitHub, Reddit, YouTube, and newsletters into one normalized, filterable view, which Jenny connects to two of her own larger builds: a similarity-and-clustering analysis across 3,000+ articles from thirteen AI newsletters, and a Substack niche-analyzer dashboard. Project 8, the tier's hardest and most complete, is shipping a full public app with authentication, Stripe subscriptions, AI content generation, an external database, and an admin dashboard — she cites her own "Quick Viral Notes" app, which reached hundreds of paying users this way, while being candid that she's since sunsetting it because the underlying AI model quality it depended on has since degraded relative to newer options. Her stated rule for this tier, based on watching builders stall here for weeks: ship the worst version that actually runs, then iterate, rather than perfecting any single piece before the loop closes.

Tier 3 reframes the goal entirely: not harder apps, but systems that keep running and compounding without daily attention. Project 9 is a personal "command center" of custom one-line commands that encode a user's actual recurring weekly workflow, built incrementally rather than all at once. Project 10 is a "living knowledge system" that captures content a reader has legitimate access to but that has no public API — Substack, Medium, Skool, Circle — through custom-built extraction tools, splitting structured findings into a database and looser material into a local vault; she built her own Substack extension this way, including premium content from writers who gave her paid-tier access. Project 11 is an autonomous, MCP-connected research pipeline chaining Perplexity for live web research, a personal knowledge base for cross-referencing, and a formatting step into a repeatable, re-runnable report generator. Project 12, framed as the tier's finale, is what she calls a "one-center ecosystem": every tool she uses (her community platform, her content scheduler, cross-posting to multiple social networks) wired through MCP so she operates from one place, describing her own working setup where drafts flow from her database to her scheduler to automatic publishing without her ever opening a browser tab. Her stated Tier 3 mistake was trying to automate workflows she'd only done a handful of times — her rule now is to do something manually at least ten times, and only automate what she can already describe in exact plain-language steps.

Tier 4 shifts again, from building owned systems to extending into tools and ecosystems a reader is already inside. Project 13 builds no-code AI agents directly inside Notion (or, alternatively, lets Claude Code use Notion as an MCP-connected memory layer) for summarizing meeting notes and updating a database automatically. Project 14 pairs n8n (for reliable routing and triggering) with Claude (for judgment calls like qualifying a lead or drafting a personalized message) into a hybrid pipeline, with the article naming the core design skill as knowing exactly which layer should own which decision. Project 15 is a "creator intelligence agent" that pulls a cohort of creators' posts via MCP and runs them through a three-pass classification, ranking, and pattern-extraction process — she reports running this on nine Substack creators and 3,230 notes, finding that the most-posted content format was actually the worst performer and that the single highest-liked note in the entire cohort was 53 words of plain text. The article closes with a downloadable cheat sheet and starter pack, an FAQ confirming the prompts are tool-agnostic (working in Cursor, Windsurf, Bolt, and Replit as well as Claude Code), and a note that the piece was updated in August 2026 to reflect newer models while keeping the tier structure and prompts intact.
