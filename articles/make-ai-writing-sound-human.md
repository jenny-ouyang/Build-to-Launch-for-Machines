# How to Make AI Writing Sound Human: The 9 Layers Most Humanize Lists Miss
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/make-ai-writing-sound-human
**Track:** AI Builders Playbook
**Published:** 2026-10-07

## Summary

Jenny opens from a real editing moment: two versions of the same explanatory passage from an AI draft of one of her own guides, one twisted and hard to parse despite plain vocabulary, the other clear after a human edit. That gap is what sends her past the usual fix for AI-sounding prose, the banned-words list. She lays out four reasons a word blacklist can never finish the job: the flagged words go stale as soon as a model generation moves past them, swapping a banned word for its "safe" replacement just creates a new word to eventually ban, several failure modes have no single word attached to them at all (an invented detail, a dropped exception, a reversed sentence), and some of the patterns a list would ban, like short punchy sentences or a repeated phrase, are sometimes exactly the effect a writer wants. Her reframing is that AI "flavor" is really an asymmetry: a tired phrase costs a reader a second, a tangled sentence costs a reread, and a factual gap or lost nuance costs the most because the reader doesn't know to go check it.

From there the article builds a nine-layer taxonomy, ordered from surface habits a reader can catch on sight to problems that only show up when a draft is checked against its own source material. The first few layers are mechanical: overused vocabulary (words like "delve" or "leverage" standing in for a plainer term), performative punctuation and formatting (em dashes, bolded bullet labels, title-case headings), and hollow sentence shapes such as the "it's not X, it's Y" contrast construction or a listicle habit of forcing a third item that adds nothing. The middle layers move into structure and tone: predictable rhythms like a warm-up paragraph before the real point or a conclusion that just restates the intro, and a performed tone, chatbot-polite or ad-copy-excited, that substitutes adjectives for actual stakes. Layer 6 covers literal leftovers, lines meant for the chat window or a tool that leaked into the final text, such as "here's a polished version" preambles, unfilled bracket placeholders, or tracking parameters left inside a link. The article is explicit that layers 1–6 are fixable by careful editing of the text alone, while layers 7–9 require outside knowledge the model doesn't have: substance (claims of importance stated instead of shown with a real number or example), invented evidence (an unnamed expert or unsourced statistic that sounds plausible but traces to nothing), and depth loss, where a rewrite keeps everything visible on the surface but quietly merges distinct ideas, drops an exception, or reverses a sentence's actors so the reader has to work out who does what.

Each layer carries a short before/after example and a correction prompt, and the article is careful to source its claims rather than assert them: a study is cited showing "delve" usage spiked and then fell in research-abstract writing once the pattern was publicly named, a 2026 preprint measuring one model generation's em-dash rate against both an earlier model and human writers, a study finding that longer AI answers alone accounted for the large majority of a measured preference-training improvement, and a widely cited 2023 study showing a reward model strongly favoring flattering answers over truthful ones, which Jenny connects to OpenAI's own 2025 rollback of an overly agreeable model update. On invented evidence, she cites OpenAI researchers' explanation that models hallucinate because training rewards a confident guess over an admission of uncertainty, and on depth loss she cites a study of science-paper summaries finding that AI summaries overgeneralized findings roughly five times as often as expert-written ones. A detector vendor's own published numbers are used against the idea that surface rewriting defeats detection outright, since even heavily "humanized" text still got flagged in all but a small fraction of cases in that vendor's test set.

The back half of the article argues that fixing all nine layers in a single AI pass actually backfires, both because a long prompt holding every rule at once gets uneven attention from a model (worse the longer the context window), and because the layers interact: trimming hedge words for layer 1 can strip out the very clauses that carry layer 9's reasoning, and asking for more specificity to fix layer 7 can invite layer 8's fabricated detail. Her proposed alternative is a three-tool division of labor: deterministic code for anything countable (exact phrases, em-dash counts, bracket leftovers), a general AI reviewer for anything that needs comprehension and can be checked against a source, and a narrower decision-only model from TypeSafe AI called Jev for judgment calls that an AI reviewer is too agreeable to flag reliably on its own. A table in the article maps each of the nine layers to which of the three tools catches it, with layers 7 through 9, substance, invented evidence, and depth loss, consistently landing back on the human author rather than any checker. Her closing point is that the goal isn't to clear every flag: some patterns are a legitimate stylistic choice she keeps on purpose, and a draft scrubbed of every flagged pattern just produces a different, voiceless kind of slop. The article closes by pointing paid subscribers to a longer companion resource, a 93-pattern reference handbook with worked examples, walkthroughs of real published-article audits, and an MCP-based reviewer skill, as the deeper version of the same framework.
