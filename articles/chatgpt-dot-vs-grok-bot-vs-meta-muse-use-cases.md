# ChatGPT dot vs Grok Bot vs Meta Muse: Who’s Your Best Personal Agent?
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/chatgpt-dot-vs-grok-bot-vs-meta-muse-use-cases
**Track:** AI Agent Systems
**Published:** 2026-10-14

## Summary

Three "personal agent" products launched within seven weeks of each other in 2026 — Grok Bot on August 11, Meta Muse on September 8, and ChatGPT dots on September 29 — and Jenny ran all three on her own real accounts on the same afternoon, October 13, under matching safety rules, to see what each one is actually good for rather than relying on launch-week hype. The article's first move is definitional: a personal agent differs from a general-purpose agent like Claude Code or Codex not in capability but in defaults. A general-purpose agent is something you point at a task; a personal agent arrives pre-connected to your own accounts and is ambient by default, waking on an email, a message, or a schedule rather than waiting to be pointed. She frames OpenClaw and Hermes as the architectural predecessors of this category — background operation, persistent memory, self-directed work — but notes the new wave trades their manual setup for one-click account connections. Each tool was tested on the cheapest plan that includes the feature: ChatGPT dots on ChatGPT Pro at $100/month, Grok Bot on Grok's $30/month premium tier, and Muse for free in the browser.

ChatGPT dots impressed her most through what ChatGPT already knew: it recalled personal facts with a source label for each (memory, a past chat, or Gmail) and proactively asked follow-up questions about gaps, ran an unsupervised 90-day Gmail sweep that surfaced genuine unused credits and refunds while explicitly flagging the limits of what email evidence can prove, built a private shareable page through ChatGPT Sites in about a minute, and handled three stacked voice requests in a single 45-second call, though it misheard both a product name and a contact's name in the process. Her verdict is that dots is the fastest path to a personal agent for anyone already living in ChatGPT, Gmail, and Codex, with the caveat that its Activity view gives no clear progress signal during a long-running task, which can read as stuck when it isn't.

Grok Bot's distinguishing trait is that it isn't one assistant but a small team: multiple named bots sharing one cloud computer, each with its own role, able to hand work to one another and check each other's output rather than just execute in isolation. It read X with zero connector setup, something neither competitor could do natively, claimed and used its own working email address (with her permission), and ran a scheduled routine that searched X in the background and then waited for her approval before sending anything. Her take is that Grok Bot is the right starting point for anyone whose work lives on X or who wants several collaborating helpers rather than one, but she flags that every bot on the account shares the same computer, browser logins, and saved passwords, so naming a bot separately creates no real security boundary, and there's no interface that shows what any bot has stored in memory.

Meta Muse was tested deliberately through the browser only, because of a separate reported incident in which a Muse Mac app allegedly synced a user's iMessage history after permission was denied, a claim Meta disputes; she wanted to see how far a low-access setup could go. It came the most pre-connected of the three, with Instagram and Threads linked from the start, and despite low expectations it produced the most complete single-chat outputs: a full podcast episode generated from an existing article and published to a real public RSS feed, a designed sales page plus a cover image and a short vertical video teaser from the same conversation, a working five-column Kanban board built from one sentence, and a one-sentence request turned into a recurring scheduled social-monitoring job. Her verdict is that Muse is the easiest way to work across the Meta apps and the best free starting point, even though its creative tools show rough edges — the video generator caps out at ten seconds and needed several attempts to render correctly.

Comparing all three, Jenny notes they converge on the same baseline capability set — a cloud computer with a browser, Gmail and calendar access, scheduled tasks, persistent memory of the user, and a permission check before sending anything — which makes routine jobs like an inbox sweep interchangeable across any of them. What separates them is each one's single exclusive capability: dots alone can hand work to Codex and publish a privately flagged page; Grok Bot alone offers native X search plus multiple named, collaborating bots with a real email address; Muse alone offers native Meta-app access and a built-in public podcast pipeline. Her closing recommendation is to pick based on where your work already lives — ChatGPT, Gmail, and Codex users should start with dots; X-centric or multi-helper workflows favor Grok Bot; Meta-app-heavy or budget-free users should start with Muse — and that if she were starting from zero with no prior subscriptions, she'd personally choose Grok Bot for its built-in multi-bot collaboration, despite not otherwise favoring Grok's models. The article closes by pointing paid subscribers to a separate Personal Agent Kit containing the exact test prompts, pass/fail results, and setup checklists behind the piece.
