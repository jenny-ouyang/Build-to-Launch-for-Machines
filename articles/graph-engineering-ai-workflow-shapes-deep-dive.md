# I Read 1,512 AI Workflows and Found 7 Shapes. You Already Use at Least One.
**URL:** https://buildtolaunch.substack.com/p/graph-engineering-ai-workflow-shapes-deep-dive
**Track:** AI Agent Systems
**Published:** 2026-10-21

## Summary

The article opens from a specific moment of friction: after Jenny demoed an automation pipeline that handled her article research, drafting and publishing with only two human decision points, an experienced collaborator described it as too dense to follow, even though that same person went on to build her own automation system shortly after. That contradiction becomes the hook for the piece's real subject, which Jenny names "graph engineering" — the idea that every piece of AI tooling people argue about (prompts, saved commands, skills, agents, loops, workflows) is the same underlying thing, a map of steps and the connections between them, dressed up with different amounts of persistence and autonomy. The piece spends its early section untangling that vocabulary before making any claims, arguing that terms like "skill" and "agent" don't nest inside each other the way people assume; a skill and a command are siblings rather than one containing the other, and a graph is just a notation for drawing the same structure, not a separate kind of object.

To test whether a handful of shapes really does explain most AI tooling, Jenny built a corpus of 1,512 SKILL.md-style workflow files pulled from 440 GitHub repositories, filtered by star count, source diversity (mixing major labs with indie builders), and topic spread so no single use case could dominate the sample. She then defined seven shapes as yes/no classification questions applied to each file: a straight-line sequence of ordered steps, a split-and-rejoin pattern where independent branches reconverge, conditional routing where one path is taken and the others are skipped, a cycle where a step sends work back to something that already ran, a stop-and-decide gate where a human must approve before work continues, a transfer of control where one unit hands the job to another without getting it back, and containment, where a single step is itself a whole sub-workflow with its own internal structure. An LLM pass scored every file against all seven questions, a method she notes cost under a quarter and took under half a minute to run across the full set.

The results, benchmarked against a separate dataset of over twelve thousand n8n automation templates as a non-AI control group, showed line and routing as near-universal (appearing in roughly 87% and 84% of files respectively), with containment showing up in nearly half the corpus and the rarest shape, transfer of control, present in only about 3% of files. Most workflow files combine several shapes rather than relying on just one or stacking all seven, and very few files were "shapeless," which she attributes to checklist-style instructions that read like workflows without actually encoding any of the seven structures. The comparison against n8n sharpened the distinction between AI-native and traditional automation: the no-code canvas tool is more reliably linear and splits work in parallel more often, while the markdown-based AI workflows branch almost twice as frequently, fold complexity behind containment roughly three times as often, and pause for a human several times more often — read as AI workflows being built more for judgment calls than for raw execution throughput.

To make the abstraction concrete, the piece walks through one real example end to end: Anthropic's own "canvas-design" skill, a 129-line Apache-licensed file that turns out to carry six of the seven shapes at once. The breakdown is used to show that the rare transfer-of-control shape isn't a minor feature there but the actual backbone of the skill's design, since it is built around handing a design philosophy from one model instance to a second one that interprets it visually. The same walkthrough catches a subtler trick: the skill never includes a genuine stop-and-decide gate, but instead fabricates a plausible human critique and writes it directly into its own instructions so it can "respond" to a objection no person actually raised, which Jenny flags as a pattern worth noticing rather than copying.

The piece closes by turning the taxonomy into a three-step exercise readers can run on their own tooling: strip an existing routine down to its bare line shape, add a single conditional branch to it, and then try nesting one skill inside another as a parent-and-child relationship. It circles back to the opening anecdote to make its verdict explicit — the session that read as overwhelming on first viewing was, in retrospect, one that moved through all seven shapes repeatedly, and the density wasn't a flaw to fix but a sign that the workflow had grown past a single shape into the vocabulary the rest of the article exists to supply.
